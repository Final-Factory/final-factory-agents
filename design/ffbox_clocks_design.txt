ffbox clocks: a deadline that is written down, and starts when the work does
============================================================================

2026-09-02.

A CI job that lands on a runner container which has been idle for 118 minutes gets two minutes to
finish, and is then stopped mid-step.

`slot.sh` sets its watchdog deadline once, at line 464, immediately after `docker run` returns:

    DEADLINE=$(( $(date +%s) + WATCHDOG_MINUTES * 60 ))

`grep -n DEADLINE runners/slot.sh` returns exactly two hits, that assignment and the comparison at
line 485. Nothing resets it. The `BUSY` flip that notices a job has started sits five lines above
the comparison, at 475, and does not touch it. So the 120 minutes is measured from CONTAINER
LAUNCH, and every minute the container spent registered and waiting for work is a minute the job
does not get.

The comment directly above the loop, at line 428, says the opposite:

    # Above main.yml's timeout-minutes: 90, so a job GitHub still wants is never killed here. This
    # exists for a container that is wedged rather than for one that is slow.

That is also what the design says, at design/ffgithubrunners_design.txt:81-83. Both are describing
a deadline that covers the job. The code has one that covers the container.

The pool made the gap wide. A quiet box holds one registered idle runner; if nobody pushes, the
watchdog recycles it at 120 minutes and a fresh one comes up in its place. Push times have nothing
to do with that cycle, so the age of the container a job lands on is roughly uniform over
[0, 120) minutes. About a quarter of jobs therefore start with less than thirty minutes of runway,
against a workflow whose own `timeout-minutes` is 90.

When it bites it does not look like a timeout. The job fails as a lost runner, with no
`timeout-minutes` message to point at; slot.sh logs "killed by the watchdog after 120 minutes"
(line 547), which reads as a wedged container rather than a healthy job that was ten minutes in;
teardown deletes the registration and systemd brings a fresh slot up, so the box looks well
afterwards. It may also leak a Unity seat, which is open item (d) in the runners design.

The second symptom is in `ffstatus.sh`. Its TTL column is blank for every CI row, because `gather()`
initialises `ttl` empty at line 301 and the `ci)` arm at 303 never fills it in. That is not an
oversight in the renderer. The CI deadline exists only as a shell variable inside one host process,
so there is nothing for a reader to read. The agent lane's spares show a TTL because
`pool-task.sh` writes `staged_at` and `ttl_secs` into `out/staged` (lines 64-65) and ffstatus
subtracts (lines 319-327).

Both symptoms have one cause. This design fixes it once, in a shape both lanes use.


1. What gets built
------------------

  1. Two named clocks in both lanes, with the same meanings on each side: an IDLE clock, which
     bounds a container that is holding a workspace and waiting for work, and a WORK clock, which
     bounds a container that has some.
  2. One rule above both: the idle clock may never fire while work is in flight. The agent lane
     has this by construction today; CI does not have it at all.
  3. A CI work clock that starts when the job starts, from a timestamp that is already on disk.
     This is the bug fix, and it restores what slot.sh:428 has always claimed.
  4. A CI idle clock, which is new: `idle_minutes`, separate from `watchdog_minutes`, bounding how
     long a registered runner waits before it is recycled onto a rebuilt image.
  5. Every deadline written down in one format, in a file, so an enforcer that restarts recovers it
     and a reader that is not the enforcer can show it.
  6. The agent idle clock moved host-side, which removes a race rather than relocating one, behind
     an in-container failsafe that keeps the property a host-side clock would otherwise lose.
  7. One shared implementation of the format and the decision rule, in `lib-workloads.sh`, which is
     already the layer both lanes share.
  8. `ffstatus` and the web page showing a TTL for CI rows, which falls out of 5 rather than being
     built.

Not a goal: one enforcer for the whole box. Section 7 is why.


2. Two clocks, and the rule that ties them
------------------------------------------

The two clocks answer different questions and want different numbers.

The IDLE clock is about staleness and cost. A container waiting for work holds RAM (about 22 GiB
for an agent spare's workspace), possibly a Unity seat, and a claim on the box's ceiling, and it
gets less useful as it sits: its workspace drifts from head, the cache entry it came from is
superseded, and `ffbox:latest` is rebuilt under it. Retiring it and staging a fresh one costs one
warm-up on a machine that by definition has nothing else to do.

The WORK clock is about a job that has stopped making progress. It is a much less frequent event
and a much more expensive one to get wrong: firing early kills work somebody is waiting for. This
is why the CI number is 120 against a workflow ceiling of 90, and why the agent lane splits its own
into three phases with separate ceilings (`ffbox:223`, `WARMUP_TIMEOUT=3600`, `AGENT_TIMEOUT=1800`,
`VERIFY_TIMEOUT=1800`), each measured from a marker the container writes rather than from launch.

The rule that ties them is the one the idle-agents design already states, in section 7:

    THE DEADLINE STOPS APPLYING AT DISPATCH. A container that has been sitting for three hours and
    fifty-nine minutes serves its prompt with the whole of `agent_secs` in front of it, exactly as
    a freshly staged one would.

That is the property CI is missing. Its single deadline does both jobs, so a container that has
been sitting for 118 minutes serves its job with two minutes in front of it.

The agent lane gets this for free because its two clocks are enforced by different things: the
container runs the idle clock and the host runs the work clocks, and the handover between them is
an atomic file create. CI has one clock in one place doing both jobs, and no handover at all.

Written out, with today's behaviour in the middle column:

    lane      clock   today                              after
    ------    -----   --------------------------------   ----------------------------------------
    agent     idle    in-container, from stage time      host-side, from stage time; failsafe kept
    agent     work    host-side, from .agent-started     unchanged
    CI        idle    (none; the work clock stands in)   host-side, from mint time
    CI        work    host-side, from CONTAINER LAUNCH   host-side, from the busy marker

Only two cells change behaviour. The rest is naming what is already there so that one reader and
one helper can serve both lanes.


3. The deadline is a file, not a variable
-----------------------------------------

Every deadline in this system is written to disk, in one format, by whoever sets it. The process
that enforces it holds no state of its own: each pass it reads the file, compares, and acts.

This is not bookkeeping for its own sake. It is the direct lesson of the CI bug's second symptom.
A deadline in a shell variable has three properties nobody chose:

  - it cannot be read by anything else, so `ffstatus` shows a blank column and an operator has no
    way to ask how long a container has left;
  - it dies with the process holding it, so a SIGKILLed `ffgithubrunners@N` leaves a container with
    no deadline at all, running until `reap.sh` notices the supervisor pid is gone on its
    fifteen-minute timer;
  - it cannot be checked by a test without running the supervisor.

The format is the one `pool-task.sh` already writes:

    staged_at=2026-09-02T14:31:07-04:00      when the clock started, ISO 8601 with an offset
    ttl_secs=14400                            how long it runs for

Two keys, and the deadline is their sum. Storing the start and the duration rather than the
absolute expiry is what lets a reader show BOTH "forty minutes left" and "this has been up for
three hours", which are the two things an operator actually asks. It also survives a config change
honestly: a container carries the TTL it was created under, which is already the documented
behaviour of `FFBOX_IDLE_TTL_SECS` (pool-task.sh:41, "the deadline a container enforces is the one
that was configured when it was staged").

Where each file lives:

    agent idle   pool/<pool id>/out/staged            exists; add nothing
    agent work   the .agent-started / .verify-started markers, plus the ceilings ffbox holds
    CI idle      state/<container>.idle               new, written by slot.sh at mint
    CI work      state/<container>.busy               EXISTS ALREADY and already carries the time

That last line is the piece that makes this cheap. `ffghr_mark_busy` (runners/lib/config.sh:608)
writes:

    printf 'at=%s\n' "$(date -Is)" > "$(ffghr_busy_marker "${1:?}")"

The moment a CI job starts is already recorded, in a file, in ISO 8601, by the supervisor, for the
pool's own accounting. The work clock does not need new state. It needs to read the file that is
already there.

The agent work clocks stay as they are. They are markers plus in-process ceilings rather than
`staged_at`/`ttl_secs` files, and converting them buys nothing: `ffbox` is the only reader, the
run is in the foreground of the process holding the ceiling, and a restart of that process means
the run is gone anyway. The rule in this section is about deadlines that outlive a poll, which the
work clock of a foregrounded run does not.


4. The CI work clock starts at the busy marker
----------------------------------------------

In the supervisor's wait loop, when `BUSY` flips, recompute the deadline:

    if [ "$BUSY" = 0 ] && ffghr_container_busy "$CNAME"; then
        BUSY=1
        DEADLINE=$(( $(date +%s) + WATCHDOG_MINUTES * 60 ))
        ...

The flip already exists at slot.sh:475 and writes the marker two lines later. This is the
direct analogue of `ffbox` measuring the agent clock from `.agent-started` rather than from
`docker run`, and after it the comment at line 428 is true for the first time.

The cost is one poll interval of slack. `ffghr_container_busy` is checked every
`POOL_POLL_SECONDS`, five seconds, until the flip, so the job clock can start up to five seconds
late. Against 120 minutes that is not worth a second mechanism.

For restart-safety the deadline is derived from the marker rather than from the moment of the flip:

    job deadline = at(state/<container>.busy) + watchdog_minutes

A supervisor that restarts while a job is running re-reads the marker and lands on the same
deadline the previous one had. Deriving it from "when I noticed" instead would give a restarted
supervisor a fresh 120 minutes, which is the same class of bug as the one this fixes.

AND WHEN THE MARKER CANNOT BE READ, THE FALLBACK IS TODAY'S RULE, not "no deadline": the
container's start time plus `watchdog_minutes`. Section 12 is the argument; the short version is
that an unbounded work clock is worse than a work clock that is merely too generous.

Two behaviours follow that are worth stating because they are changes:

A JOB CAN NOW OUTLIVE ITS CONTAINER'S IDLE DEADLINE, deliberately. A container minted at 09:00 with
`idle_minutes: 120` that takes a job at 10:58 runs until 12:58 if it needs to. The idle clock is
cancelled, not paused, exactly as the agent lane's is at dispatch. Since a CI container serves one
job and exits, cancelling and resetting are the same observable thing.

THE WATCHDOG'S MESSAGE HAS TO SAY WHICH CLOCK FIRED. Today "killed by the watchdog after 120
minutes" covers both a wedged job and a recycled idle spare, and those want different reactions
from whoever reads the journal. After this they are separate lines, and the kind goes into the
teardown record the same way `ffbox` writes `warmup`, `agent` or `verify` into `ffbox-timeout`
(ffbox:1153).


5. The CI idle clock, which is new
----------------------------------

`githubrunner.idle_minutes`, default 120, bounding how long a registered runner waits with no job.

It is not the same number as `watchdog_minutes` and should not be derived from it. The two are
sized by different things: `watchdog_minutes` is 120 because main.yml's ceiling is 90, and
`idle_minutes` is a staleness-versus-churn trade, the same one `idle_agent_ttl_secs` makes for
agent spares. Starting them at the same value is a coincidence worth keeping only until somebody
has a reason to move one.

What the idle clock is FOR, on the CI side, is the image. `ffbox-update.timer` rebuilds
`ffbox:latest` within five minutes of a push, and a registered runner goes on running whatever it
started with. The updater destroys idle CI containers on any pass that has something to merge
(update_ffbox.sh, the sweep that skips containers with a `Runner.Worker`), so on a busy repository
the image is picked up quickly and this clock rarely fires. On a quiet week it is the only thing
that recycles a runner onto a rebuilt image, which is also the thing that keeps the Actions runner
version current enough for GitHub to keep handing it jobs.

Enforcement is the same `docker stop`, and it must stay a stop rather than a kill: PID 1 in the
container is the listener, so a TERM gives it a chance to shut down cleanly, and teardown deletes
the registration either way. Whether an idle listener deregisters itself on TERM is not
established; what is established is that a clean exit after a job does, which is why teardown's
DELETE usually returns 404 (runners design section 4, step 5). There is no grace to shorten here
just because the container is idle. An idle runner has no Unity seat and nothing to harvest, but
it may have taken a job in the seconds since the last poll, so the idle path uses the same `docker
stop -t 90` as the work path.

WHICH LEAVES ONE RACE, and it is worth naming because it is the one thing the agent lane can settle
and CI cannot. GitHub may assign a job to a runner in the instant between the idle deadline firing
and the registration being deleted. There is no `out/owner` equivalent: the other party to the
handshake is GitHub, and it does not participate. The window is the length of one `docker stop`
plus one DELETE, seconds, against an idle period measured in hours, and the failure mode is one job
failing as a lost runner. It can be narrowed by deleting the registration BEFORE stopping the
container, which turns the window into the length of one API call, at the cost of a job in flight
losing its registration if the busy check was wrong. Section 16 leaves the order open; the current
teardown order (stop, then delete) is what ships until somebody measures.


6. The agent idle clock moves to the host, behind a failsafe
------------------------------------------------------------

Today `pool-task.sh` counts its own TTL down and exits, and `ffwatch` compares nothing
(README.md:1450, "the host compares nothing"; `pool_reap` at ffwatch.py:4033 only deletes the spool
directories of containers that are already gone). Because both the host and the container can
decide the spare's fate, they need an atomic arbiter, and that is what `out/owner` is: an O_EXCL
create that both sides attempt, whoever wins says what happens next (pool-task.sh:100-125).

Moving the clock to the host does not relocate that race. It REMOVES it. The host is already the
dispatcher, so retire-and-dispatch become two decisions made by one party, serialised by the
admission lock `lib-workloads.sh` already holds. The keeper walks the spool directories every pass
in `pool_reap`; adding an expiry test there is a `stat` and a subtraction per spare, on state that
is already on disk in `out/staged`.

What it costs is the property a container-held clock has and a host-held one does not: a spare
retires itself even when nothing is watching. That matters more here than it looks, because an
agent spare holds about 22 GiB and, since 2026-09-01, a Unity seat. A stopped or crash-looping
ffwatch would hold both until an operator noticed. That is exactly the weakness this design is
fixing on the CI side, and importing it into the lane that does not have it would be a poor trade.

So the container keeps its clock as a FAILSAFE, at `ttl_secs` plus a margin, and the host becomes
the enforcer of record. In normal operation the host expires the spare first and the failsafe never
runs. When the failsafe does run it means the keeper is not keeping, which is precisely when you
want a container to hand back its seat without being asked.

`out/owner` stays exactly as it is, and the keeper retires a spare the same way it dispatches into
one: win the O_EXCL create first, then act. A keeper that loses the create has lost to a container
that is already walking out, and does nothing.

THE FAILSAFE MUST NOT BE GATED ON THAT CREATE, and this is the sharp edge of the whole section.
`pool-task.sh` today, on reaching its deadline with `owner` already taken, logs "the host has
claimed this container; waiting for the job" and pushes its own deadline 900 seconds
(pool-task.sh:113-126). It then re-attempts a create that can never succeed, so it waits, and
pushes, and waits, for as long as the container lives.

Its own comment says otherwise, and the comment is the better design: "left in place rather than
disarmed so a host that claimed and then died does not leave a container waiting for ever"
(pool-task.sh:119-122). Re-arming at plus-900 is not what stops that; only exiting is. So what
follows is not a new idea imposed on that loop, it is the loop's stated intent, which the code has
never implemented because until now nothing made the case reachable. That is survivable today
because the only thing that creates `owner` from the host side is a dispatch, which delivers
`in/dispatch` about a second later. Make the keeper create `owner` on every RETIREMENT and the
same loop becomes a trap: a keeper that claims and then dies, or whose `docker stop` fails,
strands a container holding 22 GiB and a Unity seat, with its own failsafe permanently disarmed.
That is strictly worse than what this replaces, and it is reachable by one crash at the wrong
moment.

So the container's failsafe is ABSOLUTE. At `ttl_secs` plus the margin it exits, whether or not
`owner` exists, unless `in/dispatch` has actually arrived. A claim that has not been followed by a
dispatch within the margin is not a dispatch in progress; it is a host that failed. The margin has
to be long enough that a real dispatch is never cut off -- measured at about a second, against a
margin of 900 -- and short enough that a stranded seat comes back the same hour. Start at
`ttl_secs + 900`, which is the number `pool-task.sh` already uses for the same window.

The 900-second push stays for the case it was written for, a claim that IS a dispatch. What changes
is that it is no longer unbounded: it may be taken once, and the second expiry exits.

THE KEEPER'S STOP NEEDS THE 120-SECOND FLOOR, and getting this wrong would leak a Unity seat on
every retirement rather than on none. A spare has held a seat since 2026-09-01, and the trap that
gives it back runs `unity-editor -quit -returnlicense`, an editor launch that ffbox and slot.sh
both describe as taking tens of seconds: `ffbox:1146-1150` therefore floors its stop at 120
whatever `kill_grace_secs` says, and `slot.sh:492` uses 90 for the same reason. Today the container
retires itself with `exit 0`, so the trap runs with NO deadline at all. A host-side stop replaces
"unbounded" with "however long the keeper allows", and the keeper must therefore apply the same
floor ffbox does. `kill_grace_secs`, which defaults to 10, is the wrong number here: it is about an
agent ignoring SIGTERM, not about a licence round trip.

That is also a live bug in two places this design did not set out to touch. `pool_drop`
(ffwatch.py:4156) stops a staged spare with `kill_grace_secs`, and `update_ffbox.sh:432` stops one
with a hardcoded 10, and both have been doing it to seat-holding containers since 2026-09-01 --
the updater on every pass that has something to merge, which on this box can be every five minutes.
The floor argument in `ffbox:1147` was applied to the run path and never carried to these two. The
consequence is bounded rather than permanent, because machine ids are per slot and the next
container on that slot reuses the entitlement, but between the two a concurrent activation can find
no free entitlement. Fixing it is two lines and does not wait for anything else here.

One implementation constraint, from this repository's own history. Commit c693d67 is the admission
lock riding into the run and costing the daemon three minutes of every pass, during which it
drained no Discord events and sent no replies it had already composed; from Discord it looked like
Max had stopped answering. The expiry TEST is cheap. The expiry ACTION is `docker stop --timeout`,
which is not, and it must not run synchronously on the keeper's loop. Either hand it to the same
background path staging uses, or bound it the way the speculative caller bounds its lock
acquisition (`ffbox_workload_lock_acquire 30`). Nobody is waiting on a retirement, so "not this
pass" is always an acceptable answer.


7. Enforcement stays in each lane
---------------------------------

The maximal version of this design is one sweeper — ffwatch — owning idle expiry for both lanes.
It would work mechanically. `update_ffbox.sh` already destroys idle CI containers from outside, and
`slot.sh` copes with a container exiting for reasons it did not cause, because that is also what a
finished job looks like to it.

It is rejected because of what the CI lane is for. Today the two lanes share `lib-workloads.sh`,
the daemon and the box's ceiling, and nothing else. CI keeps taking jobs while ffwatch is drained,
wedged, mid-update or broken by a commit, and that independence is worth more than one fewer loop.
A central sweeper would make every CI runner's lifetime depend on the health of the Discord daemon.

So: two enforcers, one format, one decision rule. ffwatch's keeper expires agent spares;
`slot.sh` expires its own container. Neither needs to know the other exists.

This also settles what "sharing the implementation" can mean. The enforcers are in different
languages and different process shapes: the keeper is a Python daemon holding every spare, and
`slot.sh` is a POSIX shell supervisor holding exactly one container. There is no single enforcer to
write. What is shared is the format, the arithmetic and the reader, which is section 8.


8. What is actually shared
--------------------------

Four functions in `lib-workloads.sh`, in POSIX shell, on the deadline file:

    ffbox_clock_write PATH SECS      write staged_at=<now> and ttl_secs=<secs>
    ffbox_clock_start PATH           the start, as epoch seconds; empty if unreadable
    ffbox_clock_left PATH            seconds remaining; negative when expired
    ffbox_clock_expired PATH         exit 0 when it has passed

`lib-workloads.sh` is the right home because it is already the file both lanes share for admission
and counting, and because shell is callable from both sides: `slot.sh` sources it, and ffwatch
already shells out to `ffbox` routinely through `ffbox_cmd()`. A Python reimplementation in ffwatch
would be a second model of the same format, which is the pattern `update_ffbox.sh` describes
rejecting when it stopped grepping diffs for triggers.

The callers:

    pool-task.sh          ffbox_clock_write on out/staged            (replaces its two printfs)
    slot.sh               ffbox_clock_write on state/<c>.idle at mint
    slot.sh               ffbox_clock_left  on state/<c>.busy for the job deadline
    ffwatch pool_reap     ffbox_clock_expired on out/staged, via ffbox
    ffstatus.sh           ffbox_clock_left for both lanes' TTL column
    reap.sh               unchanged; orphan sweeping is a different question

`ffstatus.sh` becomes a consumer instead of carrying its own parser. Its current spare-TTL block
(lines 319-327) is a `sed` per key plus a `date -d`, written inline, and duplicating that for the
CI arm is how the two readings start to disagree — which is the thing `--json` exists to prevent
between the terminal and the web page, and which deserves the same treatment inside the script.


9. What ffstatus and the web page get
-------------------------------------

The TTL column fills in for CI rows with no new plumbing: `gather()`'s `ci)` arm reads the idle or
busy clock through the shared helper and sets `ttl`, and everything downstream already handles it.
`render_json` emits `ttl_secs` per container today, and `ffweb.py:2147` already renders it through
`fmt_ttl`, which prints `—` for null and `expired` for a negative.

Which clock a CI row shows follows its state, and the states already exist:

    waiting     the idle clock: how long before this runner is recycled
    busy        the job clock: how long before the watchdog stops it

That is more useful than one number would have been, and it is the same split the agent rows get
(`warm` and `filling` show the idle clock; a dispatched run shows none, because its ceilings are
per-phase and live in `ffbox`).

One row will now show a TTL that nothing is enforcing: a CI container whose supervisor is dead. The
container is real, the deadline in its file is real, and the process that would act on it is gone.
`ffstatus` should say so rather than counting down to nothing. The test is the one `reap.sh:62-73`
already makes, whether the pid on the `ffghr.supervisor.pid` label is alive, and the display is the
word `orphan` in place of the number.


10. The busy marker is an inference, and what that costs depends on one number
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

`ffghr_container_busy` is `docker top "$1" -o pid,comm | grep -q 'Runner\.Worker'`
(runners/lib/config.sh:623). It is an inference from a vendor process name, polled every five
seconds.

Today a missed flip costs one miscounted idle runner: the pool believes a busy container is idle
and runs one runner short until the job ends. The job itself is unaffected, because the single
deadline fires at mint plus 120 whether the flip was seen or not.

AFTER THIS CHANGE THE COST DEPENDS ENTIRELY ON `idle_minutes`. While it equals `watchdog_minutes`,
which is what section 11 ships, a missed flip lands the container on exactly today's deadline: mint
plus 120. Nothing is worse and the improvement is simply not delivered for that job. The moment
somebody lowers `idle_minutes` below `watchdog_minutes` -- which open item (c) proposes, on the
sound reasoning that its only real job is picking up a rebuilt image -- a missed flip stops being
free and starts killing jobs early, at a deadline the job never had before.

So the mitigations below are not what makes the default safe. What makes the default safe is the
two numbers being equal. The mitigations are what has to be in place BEFORE the two numbers
diverge, and open item (c) must not be closed until they are.

Two mitigations, both cheap, and the design takes both:

  1. The idle kill refuses to fire on a container whose age already exceeds `idle_minutes` by more
     than one poll interval AND which has no busy marker but does have a `Runner.Worker`-shaped
     process under any name. Cheap version: re-check `docker top` immediately before the stop, and
     treat any exec'd process under the listener as work in flight.
  2. The stop is a stop, with the full grace. A job killed in error still gets its licence trap.

What would remove the fragility rather than bounding it is a signal the runner itself emits. The
listener logs job acceptance to its own log, which slot.sh already tees to
/var/log/ffgithubrunners/slot-N.log, so a line match there is a second and independent source. It
is not free — the log format is the vendor's too — and it is left as open item (b).


11. Configuration
-----------------

    githubrunner:
      watchdog_minutes  120     bounds a job, from the busy marker. Above main.yml's 90.
      idle_minutes      120     bounds a registered runner with no job, from mint.

    ffagent / ffdev:
      idle_agent_ttl_secs 14400 unchanged in meaning; now enforced by the keeper, with the
                                container failing safe at this plus 900.

`watchdog_minutes` keeps its name and its number. Its meaning changes from "container lifetime" to
"job lifetime", which is what its own comment and the runners design have always said it was.

`idle_minutes` is new and defaults to the same 120 the single clock effectively enforced, so a box
that upgrades and changes nothing sees the same idle recycling it saw before, and only the job
runway changes.

Both are read by `lib/config.sh` through `_ffghr_set`, alongside `watchdog_minutes` at line 137,
and both belong in the `githubrunner` section of `~/.config/ffbox/config.json` like everything else
that lane reads since 2026-09-01.

`watchdog_minutes` HAS A SECOND CONSUMER, and it is not the watchdog. `ffghr_cache_should_archive`
(runners/lib/config.sh:471-475) ages out a cache claim with it: "a claim older than the watchdog
belongs to a job that is gone". Re-basing the watchdog on the job rather than the container leaves
that reader correct, and arguably more correct, because a claim is made BY a job and is now bounded
by the same clock the job is. What it does change is the ceiling on a container's total life, which
goes from `watchdog_minutes` to `idle_minutes + watchdog_minutes`; nothing reads that, but anybody
reasoning about how long an `ffghr-*` container can exist has been able to say "two hours" until
now and cannot after.

THE TWO NUMBERS NEED COERCING, not just defaulting. `idle_minutes` at 0 is a legitimate wish (never
recycle an idle runner) and must be read as "no idle deadline" rather than as "expire immediately",
which is the coercion both pools already apply to a `pool.idle` of 0. A negative value is 0. And a
very small value is a way to churn registrations against GitHub's API without meaning to, so the
floor below which the config is refused rather than honoured is worth setting at the point the
value is read.


12. Failure modes
-----------------

THE SUPERVISOR DIES WITH A JOB RUNNING. Its deadline is in `state/<c>.busy`, so a restarted
supervisor recovers it. If the supervisor does not come back, nothing enforces the job clock and
`reap.sh` sweeps the container within fifteen minutes of its pid going away. That is a change in
kind from today, where the deadline is simply lost.

THE KEEPER DIES WITH SPARES STAGED. The container failsafe fires at `ttl_secs + 900` and the spare
retires itself, returning its Unity seat through the `trap return_license EXIT INT TERM` that
`unity-license.sh:115` arms and `pool-task.sh` inherits as pid 1. This is section 6's whole reason
for keeping the failsafe.

THE KEEPER CLAIMS `owner` AND THEN DIES, or its `docker stop` fails. The container sees a claim
that never becomes a dispatch, takes its one 900-second push, and exits at the second expiry
rather than waiting for a job that is not coming. Without the absolute failsafe of section 6 this
is the case that strands a container for ever, and it is the single worst outcome available in
this design.

THE BUSY MARKER IS MISSED. Section 10. Bounded by the pre-stop re-check, not eliminated.

A RETIREMENT IS STOPPED TOO FAST TO RETURN ITS SEAT. The keeper applies ffbox's 120-second floor
rather than `kill_grace_secs`; below that the container is SIGKILLed part-way through
`-returnlicense` and the seat is held until the slot's machine id is reused. Section 6.

THE CLOCK FILE IS UNREADABLE OR MALFORMED, AND THE TWO CLOCKS FAIL IN OPPOSITE DIRECTIONS. This is
the one place where a single rule would be wrong.

For an IDLE clock, empty means no deadline and nothing is stopped. A missing file must never mean
kill: the file is written at mint, and a write failure there is already non-fatal for the busy
marker (slot.sh:480-482 logs and continues). The cost of failing this way is a container that
lives too long, which the next update pass or a restart clears.

For a WORK clock, no deadline means a WEDGED CONTAINER RUNS FOR EVER, holding a slot, a workspace
and a Unity seat -- strictly worse than today, where it dies at mint plus 120. So the work clock
falls back to the container's own start time plus `watchdog_minutes`, read from the daemon, which
is exactly today's rule and is always bounded. The busy marker is an optimisation on top of that
fallback, never the only source of it.

That fallback also covers the deploy window: a marker written by the old supervisor carries no
`ttl_secs`, the helper returns empty, and the job is bounded by the launch-based deadline it would
have had anyway.

A CONFIG CHANGE MID-LIFE. A container carries the TTL it was created under, because `ttl_secs` is
in its file. Lowering `idle_minutes` does not retire anything already up, for the same reason
lowering `idle_agents` does not: it is a target, not a headcount enforced downwards.

THE IDLE KILL RACES A JOB ASSIGNMENT. Section 5. Seconds wide, one job fails as a lost runner,
narrowable by reordering teardown, left open.


13. Where this is worse than what it replaces
---------------------------------------------

Four places, and one of them is a whole phase.

THE AGENT LANE GETS NO REPAIR, ONLY A REFACTOR. Section 6 moves a clock that works. The race it
removes has produced no failure anybody has recorded: `out/owner` was written correctly the first
time and has settled every dispatch since. What the move buys is one fewer party able to decide a
spare's fate, and a second lane described in the same vocabulary as the first. What it costs is a
new host-side stop path, a keeper that can strand a container if the failsafe is built wrong, and a
lane whose RAM and Unity seats now come back on time only while ffwatch is healthy. The failsafe
puts a floor under that, and the floor is the margin rather than the deadline: a keeper that dies
at the wrong moment turns a four-hour spare into a four-hour-and-fifteen-minute one. That is a fair
price for a simplification and a poor one for a repair, and this is a simplification. If only part
of this design is built, section 6 is the part to leave out.

TWO NUMBERS CAN BE SET AGAINST EACH OTHER. Today `watchdog_minutes` cannot be inconsistent with
anything, because it is the only number there is. After this, `idle_minutes` below
`watchdog_minutes` makes the busy-flip inference load-bearing for whether a job survives
(section 10), and `idle_minutes` far above it makes a runner that GitHub stopped offering work to
sit registered for a day. Neither is wrong enough to refuse, both are worth a line in the README,
and the coercion in section 11 is what stops the obviously broken values.

MORE STATE THAT CAN LIE. A `.idle` marker per container, a `retiring` state, an `orphan` display,
and a TTL column that now carries a number for CI rows. Today that column is blank, which is
honest: nothing knew the answer. A number read from a file the enforcer may not be reading is worse
than a blank, which is why section 9 spends a paragraph on the orphan case and section 12 on the
unreadable one. Every one of these is a new opportunity for the page and the box to disagree.

A SEAT RETURN GAINS A DEADLINE IT DID NOT HAVE. Today a spare retiring itself runs
`-returnlicense` from an EXIT trap with no clock on it at all. Under host-side expiry it gets
whatever the keeper's `docker stop` allows, and the design sets that at ffbox's 120-second floor.
That is generous against a round trip measured in tens of seconds, and it is still a bound where
there was none. A licence server having a slow day now costs a seat that self-retirement would
have waited out.

A CONTAINER CAN LIVE TWICE AS LONG. `idle_minutes` then `watchdog_minutes` end to end is up to four
hours where the ceiling has been two. Nothing reads that ceiling, but people do: "an ffghr
container exists for at most two hours" has been a true sentence when reasoning about Unity seat
exposure, about how long a JIT registration can sit on the org's runner page, and about cache
claims. It stops being true, and the replacement sentence is longer.

ONE THING THAT LOOKS WORSE AND IS NOT. The idle-expiry race with a job assignment (section 5) is
not new. Today's watchdog stops an idle container at mint plus 120 and can collide with an
assignment in flight in exactly the same way, with exactly the same outcome. This design neither
creates that race nor removes it, and it should not be counted against it.


14. What this does not do
-------------------------

It does not add a second clock to an agent RUN. The three phase ceilings in `ffbox` are already the
right shape and already start at markers.

It does not make the two lanes share an enforcer, a process, or a language. Section 7.

It does not change what happens at expiry: `docker stop` with a grace, never `docker kill`, in both
lanes, because in both lanes PID 1 has a trap that returns a Unity seat.

It does not touch `reap.sh`. Orphan sweeping asks "is the supervisor gone", which is a different
question from "has the deadline passed", and the two want different answers.

It does not give ffwatch any role in the CI lane, or `slot.sh` any role in the agent lane.

16. Open questions
------------------

  a. Teardown order on an idle expiry: stop then delete (today), or delete then stop, which
     narrows the assignment race at the cost of a mis-detected busy container losing its
     registration. Wants one measurement of how GitHub answers a DELETE for a runner it has just
     assigned a job to.
  b. Whether the runner's own log gives a durable job-start signal, which would replace the
     `Runner.Worker` inference in section 10 rather than bounding it.
  c. Whether `idle_minutes` should default lower than 120 now that it no longer doubles as the job
     bound. Its only real job is picking up a rebuilt image on a quiet week, and the updater
     already does that within five minutes on any week that has a push. NOT CLOSEABLE ON ITS OWN:
     lowering it below `watchdog_minutes` is what turns a missed busy flip from free into a killed
     job (section 10), so (b) or the pre-stop re-check has to be in place and watched first.
  d. Whether the agent failsafe margin of 900 is right, or whether it should scale with `ttl_secs`.
     It is now doing two jobs: covering a dispatch in flight, which takes about a second, and
     bounding how long a stranded claim holds a Unity seat, which wants to be short. Those pull in
     the same direction, so 900 is probably generous rather than wrong, but the second job is new
     with this design and 900 was chosen for the first.
  e. Unchanged from the runners design: whether the watchdog's TERM reaches the licence trap
     through the runner. This design makes the watchdog fire less often on real jobs, which lowers
     the stakes but does not answer it.
