# Plain writing

Ben's rules, applied to anything written for a human reader. They are about removing the
statistical signature of machine-written prose, not about being terse. Readable beats short.

Genre still governs tone. Technical, reference, legal, and academic writing stays plain and
neutral. Personal writing, a talk, or a summary for one person can carry opinion, uncertainty,
first person, and humour.

## Drafting rules

- Use ordinary, precise words. Pick the plain one over the academic or inflated one.
- Use `is`, `has`, and `does` when they are the clearest verbs available.
- Vary sentence length the way normal writing does. Mix short and longer sentences, but do not
  manufacture dramatic one-line punchlines.
- Use active voice where it names the actor more clearly.
- Prefer the concrete detail you were given over an abstraction that stands in for it.
- Organise around what the reader needs, not around an essay template.

## Habits to avoid

**Inflation.** Ordinary facts described as pivotal moments, testaments, broader shifts, or
evidence of an evolving landscape.

**Promotional filler.** Groundbreaking, seamless, vibrant, breathtaking, unlock, empower,
revolutionise. Only if the evidence in front of you actually supports the word.

**AI vocabulary.** Delve, tapestry, intricate, interplay, underscore, showcase, foster, crucial,
pivotal, leverage (as a verb), robust, comprehensive. Use the plainer word.

**Vague authority.** "Experts say", "observers note", "research shows", "studies suggest",
"it is widely regarded" without naming who.

**Significance clauses.** Trailing `-ing` phrases that pretend to explain why something matters:
highlighting, showcasing, ensuring, reflecting, underscoring, demonstrating. Cut them or replace
them with the actual consequence.

**Recycled constructions.** "Not only X but also Y", "it's not X, it's Y", "from X to Y",
"X isn't just Y". One of these in a document is fine. Three is a signature.

**Forced triads.** Three examples because three sounds complete, rather than because there are
three. Use two or four when that is the true count.

**Synonym cycling.** Swapping in a worse word to avoid repeating the clearest one. Repeat the
right term.

**Hedging and throat-clearing.** "It is important to note", "in order to", "at this point in
time", "could potentially", "it should be mentioned", "generally speaking".

**Structure announcements.** "Let's dive in", "here's a breakdown", "let's explore", "in this
section we will". Start instead.

**Empty openings and closings.** An introduction that restates the title, or a conclusion that
summarises what the reader just read without adding anything.

**Chatbot residue.** "Great question", "I hope this helps", "let me know if you'd like more",
offers to continue, apologies for length.

**Formatting excess.** Too many headings, bold runs, bullet lists, emoji, title-case headings.
Prose in paragraphs is often the right answer.

**Rhythm tells.** Repeated em dashes, paragraphs of identical shape and length, uniform sentence
length, unbroken polished cadence. Em dashes are the most reliable single tell; a document with
one every other sentence reads as machine-written regardless of content.

**Manufactured voice.** Fake intimacy, rhetorical questions the writer immediately answers,
aphorisms, and sentences built to be quotable. Also the opposite failure: deliberate typos,
inserted slang, or broken grammar added to seem human.

## Editing pass (required, silent)

Run this before delivering. Do not narrate it or report on it.

1. Delete any sentence that could be pasted into an unrelated document without changing much.
2. Replace abstractions with the specifics you were actually given.
3. Remove claims you cannot support and attributions you cannot name.
4. Cut redundant introductions, summaries, and conclusions.
5. Check for repeated sentence shapes and rhetorical patterns. Count the em dashes.
6. Ask what still reads as generic or machine-written, and rewrite those passages without
   changing any fact.

## Applying it to slides

Slides amplify every habit above, because a headline has room for exactly one idea and the
temptation is to make that idea sound profound.

- A headline states what the slide shows. It is not a thesis, a slogan, or a line the audience
  is meant to remember.
- Body text is for a reader who is also listening to someone talk. Short, plain, skippable.
- If a card or bullet list has three items, check that three is the real number.
- Pull quotes and callout boxes attract aphorisms. Put a fact in them instead.
